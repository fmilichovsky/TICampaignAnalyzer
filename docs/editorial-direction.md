# Editorial direction

What a campaign history is for, what it emphasizes and what it plays down. Terms are defined in
[CONTEXT.md](../CONTEXT.md).

## Purpose

- **An alternate history of the world, as a well-informed public saw it.** It reads as an amalgamation of what
  journalists reported, what historians later concluded and what the public came to believe. A save knows what
  changed; only this vantage turns it into a history someone would read.
- **Its interest is how the themes interleave.** A war fought over launch sites, an off-world industry that stops
  needing Earth's money, an alien threat that excuses a rearmament. Told apart, each theme is a list; the campaign
  is in how one moved another.
- **A shocking turn is framed, not only reported.** A great power disarming, old rivals merging, a war that stops
  overnight: the history says how the turn was presented, what observers noticed behind it and what the public
  settled on. A turn that defies conventional expectations reads as a glitch until it is explained the way its own
  world would have explained it. The framing is interpretation and never changes what happened
  ([ADR-0014](adr/0014-the-llm-narrates-and-never-reconstructs.md)).
- **It tells developments.** It never lists events or the data behind them, because a reader recalls what changed
  in the world, not the records that state it.
- **It looks back.** It gives no strategy advice, no plausibility notes and no forecast: those address a player,
  and the reader is not one.

## One vantage: the public's

- **The history is the world's.** The player's faction earns space only by impact. The player drives the largest
  changes, so playstyle shows by itself; a rule that weighed it up would count it twice.
- **A hidden hand shows as a pattern.** The same backer behind every campaign, a reversal too fast to be natural,
  a silence that is noted. The public never saw who directed a change, only that something did.

## It follows the themes

- **The themes are not ranked.** Which of them carries a campaign depends on how it was played.
- **A thin theme stays thin.** A campaign that barely touched a theme is told that way; the gap says something
  about the campaign and is never padded out.
- The themes are named and described once, in the **Theme** entry of [CONTEXT.md](../CONTEXT.md#calibration).

## What it plays down

- **Routine churn**: small-state turnovers, councilor traffic, statistic drift. Every campaign has it and it
  distinguishes none.
- **The steps of an episode**, in favour of its outcome: a continent reunified, not each war that reunified it.
  The outcome is what a reader recalls.
- **Repeats.** An episode is told once; a second telling adds length and no history.
- **Game mechanics**: control points, arithmetic, record dates. The public never saw them.
- **A faction as the visible actor.** The public sees governments, blocs and fleets act. Who steered them is at
  most what observers suspect.

## What each level means to a reader

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| campaign-defining | The history can't be told without it |
| major | An episode the history tells, one a reader would recall |
| minor | Detail on demand; colour the history may use and never has to |
| trivial | Evidence for the levels above it |

- **There is no weak major.** Everything at a level meets that level's meaning in full. An event that is almost an
  episode worth recalling is a minor one, because a reader cannot tell a marginal entry from a real one and reads
  both as history.

## No density, at any level

- **Each event is judged by itself.** A quiet stretch is told briefly and a crowded one at length. A history paced
  evenly would pad the one and cut the other, and neither would be what happened.
